Physical culture elders: what the old strongmen and Muscle Beach regulars kept doing

Twenty strongmen, strongwomen, wrestlers and Muscle Beach regulars from roughly the 1880s to the 1960s: what they practiced, and what they were still doing later in life. History and inspiration, not medical advice.

Start here: This is a scrapbook, not a study. History tends to remember the people who lived a long time and forget the ones who didn't (that's survivorship bias), so a list like this can make any habit look like a secret. It isn't. Several famous strongmen died young, and the best known of them all didn't reach 60 (see the Sandow note below). Read these as twenty real lives with a few practices worth noticing: walking, short bodyweight routines, grip work, play, plain food, and easing off after the peak years instead of stopping.

How the facts were checked

Solid Obituaries, newspapers or scholarly sources agree.

Reported Credible secondary sources, but thin or self-reported. Marked "reported" in the text.

Legend Showman stories or later retellings with no primary evidence. Labeled as legend, or left out.

Bronze era (roughly 1880s–1930s)

George Hackenschmidt, "The Russian Lion"

1877–1968 · reached 90 Solid

World heavyweight wrestling champion in the early 1900s, strongman, the name behind the hack squat, and later an author on physical culture (The Way to Live, 1908).

  • He kept jumping as a favorite leg exercise, and later on he argued against drilled, one-size-fits-all systems.
  • Shifted from meat-eating to a strict vegetarian diet later in life.

As the story goes: one biographer's account has him jumping over a chair in his mid-80s. It's repeated widely but thinly sourced.

Bernarr Macfadden

1868–1955 · reached 87 Solid

Publisher of Physical Culture magazine and a loud promoter of exercise, walking and "natural" living.

  • Organized long group walks and did calisthenics, weights and headstands (reported).
  • Parachute jumps for his 81st, 83rd and 84th birthdays, and a headstand for press photographers at 75.

See the caution box: a colorful figure, not a guide for medical decisions.

Joseph Pilates

1883–1967 · reached 83 Solid

Creator of "Contrology," today's Pilates method: mat work plus apparatus he designed, taught from his studio on 8th Avenue in New York.

  • Daily controlled mat sequences and breathing work.
  • Still teaching at his studio into his 80s.

Stanislaus Zbyszko

c. 1879/80–1967 · reached about 87–88 (birth year disputed) Solid

World heavyweight wrestling champion in the 1920s, highly educated and multilingual, remembered for a famous 1928 match against Gama.

  • Late in life he lived on a farm near Savannah, Missouri, with his brother Wladek, training young wrestlers, including Harley Race and Johnny Valentine.

Thomas Inch

1881–1963 · reached about 81–82 Solid

Billed as "Britain's Strongest Man" in the early 1900s, owner of the thick-handled Inch "unliftable" challenge dumbbell (about 172 lb), and a mail-order course seller.

  • Heavy grip and thick-bar work.
  • Reported to have coached local teenagers in weightlifting in Cobham later in life.

Legend: that he could lift his own 172 lb dumbbell overhead at will. Historians question it.

J.P. Müller

1866–1938 · reached about 71–72 Solid

Author of My System (1904), a 15-minutes-a-day, no-equipment routine translated into dozens of languages and one of the first mass-market home fitness books.

  • 18 simple exercises (trunk circles, bends, twists, leg swings, breathing), built up over six weeks and finished with a cold bath and brisk rub-down.

Not a long-life standout. He's here for the most borrowable idea on the list: a short daily routine. The book is free to read.

Katie Sandwina

1884–1952 · reached 68 Solid

Circus strongwoman of the Ringling / Barnum & Bailey era, famous for pressing her husband overhead, and a supporter of women's suffrage.

  • Reported to have kept doing strength demonstrations (bending bars, breaking chains, pressing her husband) at the bar she ran in Queens after leaving the circus.

Not a long-life story. A strong-into-her-60s story, and one of the few well-documented women of the era.

Charles Atlas

1892–1972 · reached 80 Solid

Seller of the "Dynamic Tension" mail-order course from the 1920s on, billed as the "World's Most Perfectly Developed Man."

  • Self-resistance exercises, muscle against muscle, no equipment. Reported to have kept up his routine into his later years.

Legend: the "97-pound weakling" origin story came from comic-book ads. Advertising history, not biography.

Joseph Greenstein, "The Mighty Atom"

1893–1977 · reached 84 Solid

A small strongman (about 5 ft 4 in) who bent horseshoes and bars and bit through nails and chains.

  • Still performing in his 80s. His last public show is widely reported at Madison Square Garden (sources differ on 1976 or 1977).

Legend: the stories about surviving a bullet to the forehead and stopping a plane with his hair come from family and biographer tellings.

Siegmund Klein

1902–1987 · reached 85 Solid

Ran his 7th Avenue gym in Manhattan for about 45 years; strongman, physique pioneer, writer and publisher.

  • Reported, from his own writing: stopped chasing heavy records around 35 and switched to moderate, roughly hour-long sessions about three times a week, avoiding training to exhaustion.

The best documented "moderation" voice on the list.

Joe Rollino, "The Great Joe Rollino"

1905–2010 · reached 104 Solid

1920s Coney Island strongman (about 5 ft 5 in, 150 lb), boxer, and decorated WWII veteran.

  • A daily early-morning walk was a lifelong routine.
  • Still bending quarters with his fingers at 104. He died after being struck by a minivan while out on that morning walk.

Legend: he was billed as lifting 3,200 lb. That's a showman's claim, not a record.

Silver era (roughly 1940s–1960s)

John Grimek, "The Monarch of Muscledom"

1910–1998 · reached 88 Solid

1936 US Olympic weightlifter, the first two-time AAU Mr. America (1940, 1941), and a longtime magazine editor. Known for flexibility and hand-balancing alongside the muscle.

  • At 64, frequent lighter basement workouts plus 45–60 minutes of evening cycling.
  • His advice for older lifters: cut the weight, sets or intensity when recovery lags, but stay active.

Jack LaLanne, "Godfather of Fitness"

1914–2011 · reached 96 Solid

Opened one of the first modern US gyms (Oakland, 1936) and hosted The Jack LaLanne Show on TV from 1951.

  • Into his 90s, reported daily training of about two hours: roughly 90 minutes of weights plus swimming or walking.
  • At 70, swam about a mile to a mile and a half in Long Beach Harbor, handcuffed and shackled, towing 70 boats.

The blender drink tied to his name is on era training drinks.

Elaine LaLanne, "First Lady of Fitness"

Born 1926 · 100 and living (as of spring 2026) Solid

Co-host of The Jack LaLanne Show, author, and 2026 Health & Fitness Association Hall of Fame inductee alongside Jack. She quit smoking and her daily donut after meeting Jack in 1951.

  • At 100, per recent interviews: jackknife ab exercises before getting out of bed, leg lifts lying down, and push-ups against a wall or sink.
  • No named diet: yogurt, berries, salmon or chicken, lots of vegetables, and cocoa instead of coffee.

Abbye "Pudgy" Stockton, "First Lady of Iron"

1917–2006 · reached 88 Solid

Queen of the original Muscle Beach. Her "Barbelles" column in Strength & Health (1944–1954) promoted weight training for women.

  • Weights plus hand-balancing and acrobatics on the sand with her husband, Les Stockton.

Verified details about her later training are scarce, so none are guessed at here.

Armand Tanny

1919–2009 · reached 90 Solid

Muscle Beach regular, 1949 Pro Mr. America and 1950 Pro Mr. USA, then decades as a writer for Weider magazines.

  • Muscle Beach-era weights, hand-balancing and wrestling (reported).
  • Reported to have kept writing as a senior writer into old age.

Steve Reeves

1926–2000 · reached 74 Solid

Mr. America 1947, Mr. Universe 1950, and the star of Hercules (1958).

  • Classic full-body training about three times a week.
  • Later wrote Powerwalking (1982): long strides, arm swing and rhythmic breathing, a style he worked out walking his horses on his ranch.

Not a long-life story. The takeaway is walking as a lifelong practice.

Bill Pearl

1930–2022 · reached 91 Solid

Four-time Mr. Universe (1953–1971), author of the Keys to the Inner Universe training encyclopedia, gym owner and coach.

  • High-volume, early-morning training for decades; said at 78 that he still trained daily.
  • Lacto-ovo vegetarian (eggs and dairy, no meat) from about 39.

Joan Rhodes, "The Mighty Mannequin"

1921–2010 · reached 89 (90 if born in 1920, as some sources say) Solid

Post-war variety strongwoman who tore phone books, bent nails and bars, and lifted Bob Hope. Abandoned as a child, she built a stage career from nothing.

  • Joined a weightlifting club at 16.
  • After the stage: small film roles, painting, poetry, a magazine column, and an autobiography, Coming On Strong (2006).

In her words: "A lot of my strength came from spirit."

Clarence "Clancy" Ross

1923–2008 · reached 84 Solid

AAU Mr. America 1945, Pro Mr. America 1946 and Mr. USA 1948; a classic Silver-era physique.

  • Reported to have run gyms in the East Bay for decades.

The thinnest record of the twenty. Verified detail about his later training is scarce.

Practices that recur

These are patterns that show up across these lives, not causes of anything. Take what's useful.

  • Walking, most days. Rollino's morning walk, Macfadden's group walks, Reeves's powerwalking, LaLanne's walking and swimming. For a change of direction indoors, see backwards walking.
  • Short daily bodyweight routines. Müller's 15 minutes, Elaine LaLanne's ab work before getting out of bed, Atlas's Dynamic Tension, Pilates's mat work.
  • Backing off after the peak years instead of quitting. Klein moved to moderate sessions around 35. Grimek went lighter in his 60s and said to cut the load when recovery lags, but keep moving.
  • Grip and odd objects. Inch's thick-handled dumbbell, Greenstein's horseshoes, Rollino's quarters, Sandwina's bars and chains, Rhodes's phone books. A modern, friendlier version: a garage sandbag.
  • Play and hand-balancing. Pudgy Stockton and the Muscle Beach crowd on the sand, Hackenschmidt's jumping, Grimek's flexibility and hand-balancing.
  • Simple food, with personal quirks. Hackenschmidt's later vegetarianism, Pearl's eggs-and-dairy vegetarian diet, the LaLannes' plain whole foods. Era recipes live on era training drinks.
  • Teaching late in life. Zbyszko training young wrestlers on his farm, Inch coaching teenagers, Klein running his gym, Pilates teaching into his 80s, Tanny still writing.
Not everything is worth copying. These people weren't perfect, and two habits here are history only.
  • Fasting as medicine. Macfadden promoted fasting, and several of his health claims were pseudoscience. In 1955 he tried to treat a urinary blockage by fasting and died days after being hospitalized. Enjoy the parachute stunts; don't take medical cues from him.
  • Cigars. Joseph Pilates was reported to be a lifelong cigar smoker. That's a fact about him, not a habit to borrow.
Not medical advice. Talk to a qualified professional about anything health-related.

The contrast: Eugen Sandow (1867–1925)

The father of modern bodybuilding, and the most famous strongman of all, died at 58. As the story goes, he died after lifting a car out of a ditch. That story is disputed: records point to an aortic aneurysm or a brain hemorrhage, and there was no autopsy. He's a good reminder that this page is about practices worth noticing, not predictions.

Free reading

Two of the original books are public domain and free to read on the Internet Archive:

  • J.P. Müller, My System: the 15-minutes-a-day routine, with the exercises laid out step by step.
  • George Hackenschmidt, The Way to Live: his book on health and physical fitness (this scan is a 1911 printing).

Read them as period pieces, not current advice.

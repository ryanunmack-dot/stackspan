Home movement kit: what to buy first, and in what order

By StackSpan Editors

A small kit for moving more at home, in the order it's worth buying: nothing at first, then bands, one weight and barefoot shoes, then rings, a ball and a timer. Everything else is nice to have. Picks are researched from specs, listings and coaching material, not hands-on tested. Not medical advice.

Inspired by Joseph Bartz's equipment tier list (his full list here). The order, picks and opinions on this page are StackSpan's own.

Start here: Gear doesn't start a practice. Moving does. Spend the first month buying nothing, then add one thing at a time and only keep what gets used every week.
Start easy. Stop if something hurts sharply. Check anchors (beams, branches, door frames) before hanging any weight from them, and clear the floor before swinging or juggling anything. Not medical advice.

Step 1 · month one

Buy nothing

  • Floor: get down to the floor and back up a few different ways, sit in different positions, crawl a little.
  • Walking: most days, with a hill or a few flights of stairs when they're on the way.
  • Carrying household things: grocery bags, a laundry basket, a backpack with books. Carry, set down, switch sides.
  • Free hanging and balance: a sturdy playground bar to hang from, a curb or low rail to balance on.

If the habit holds for a month, the gear below has something to attach to.

Step 2 · next

Bands, one weight, barefoot shoes

Long resistance bands Researched

Why it earns a spot: Light, cheap and easy to pack. A band pulls from whatever direction it is anchored, not only straight down like a weight, so it can load angles that bodyweight and dumbbells miss.

  • Band pull-aparts at chest height, slow on the way back.
  • Rows with the band looped around a post or a sturdy railing.
  • Band-assisted hangs or pull-ups, with the band under a foot or knee to take some weight.

Budget / DIY: One medium long-loop band is enough to start; add a lighter and a heavier one later. Bands wear out over the years, so check for nicks before each session.

Adjustable dumbbell Researched

Why it earns a spot: A plain spin-lock dumbbell is cheap, small and adjustable. A light load (well under what most people call lifting) turns familiar bodyweight moves into something new.

  • Goblet squats, holding one end of the dumbbell at the chest.
  • One-arm rows with a hand on a chair or bench.
  • Suitcase carry: one dumbbell in one hand, walk the hallway, switch hands.

Budget / DIY: Check secondhand listings first. Lots of people sell barely used dumbbells. One dumbbell is enough to start.

Check price

Barefoot shoes Researched

For walking and low-impact work, a wide, flat, flexible shoe keeps the feet close to the floor with clearer feedback underfoot. A cushioned running shoe still has a place for running and high-impact days. The shoe pages cover it: beginner barefoot shoes, barefoot vs traditional, and how to transition gradually.

Step 3 · then

Rings, a ball, a timer

Gymnastic rings Researched

Why it earns a spot: Two independent handles that hang from a beam, a pull-up bar or a solid tree branch. They pack into a small bag and scale from simple hangs to harder work for years.

  • Hangs with the feet on the ground for help, then without.
  • Ring rows: body leaning back, feet forward, pull the chest to the rings.
  • Support hold at the top of a dip, rings close to the sides.

Budget / DIY: Just hanging is a fine start. Wooden rings with long cam-buckle straps adjust for any anchor; check that the beam or branch is sound before putting full weight on it.

Check price

Tennis ball Researched

Why it earns a spot: Costs almost nothing, fits in a pocket, and works alone or with a partner at any level.

  • Throw and catch against a wall, one hand, then the other.
  • Learn a two-ball, then three-ball juggle.
  • Partner catch games with made-up rules: weak hand only, catch while standing on one foot.

Budget / DIY: Old, dead tennis balls work fine for play. A can of regular-duty balls covers a household.

Check price

Interval timer (Gymboss) Researched

Why it earns a spot: A tiny clip-on timer that beeps through intervals, so the phone can stay in the bag and the session stays on track.

  • Simple intervals: 40 seconds of work, 20 seconds easy, for 10 minutes.
  • Timed hangs on the rings, a little longer each week.
  • A walk-and-carry block: switch hands at every beep.

Budget / DIY: A kitchen timer, or a phone left on the far side of the room.

Step 4 · later

Nice to have

Worth it once the basics are a habit, or when a specific practice calls for them.

Liquid chalk Researched

Why it earns a spot: Dry hands hold on longer on rings and bars. Liquid chalk goes on once and doesn't dust up a bag or a car.

  • One thin coat before a session of hangs or ring work.
  • Bar and ring work on humid days.
  • Climbing sessions.

Budget / DIY: Skip it until sweaty hands cut sessions short. Training without it some of the time keeps the grip honest.

Check price

Athletic tape Researched

Why it earns a spot: Cheap, and it covers a hot spot on the palm or wrist so a ring or bar session can keep going.

  • Wrap a palm or wrist hot spot before hangs.
  • A quick wrap over a small cut so a session isn't lost.
  • Low-stick tape that peels off cleanly after a session.

Budget / DIY: Any cheap roll of white cotton sports tape. A multi-pack lasts a long time.

Check price

Kettlebell Researched

Why it earns a spot: One bell covers swings, squats and carries, and the offset handle feels different from a dumbbell. It costs more per kilo than a dumbbell, and a range of sizes adds up, so it comes after the dumbbell.

  • Two-hand swings, learned slowly from a qualified coach or a well-regarded tutorial.
  • Goblet squats holding the bell by the horns.
  • Rack or suitcase carries around the yard.

Budget / DIY: One moderate bell, secondhand if possible. A second bell can wait.

Check price

Foam roller Researched

Why it earns a spot: An easy excuse to spend time on the floor. Many people use one less once rolling around on the floor itself becomes part of the routine.

  • Slow rolls along the upper back, hands behind the head.
  • Lie lengthwise along the roller and let the arms open out to the sides.
  • Use it as a prop for floor play, then put it away.

Budget / DIY: A lacrosse ball for small spots, or just rolling around on a rug.

Wood blocks (Jenga-style) Researched

Why it earns a spot: A cheap way to practice dexterity: fine, careful control of hands and body, which most training skips.

  • Balance a block on the head and walk, then squat slowly.
  • Stack two blocks in a T on one palm and move the arm around.
  • Partner game: blocks placed on the floor around you, pick them up without moving your feet.

Budget / DIY: Cut short blocks from a scrap 2x4 and sand the edges.

Check price

Mobility stick (dowel) Researched

Why it earns a spot: Cheap and light, and useful for slow overhead and hip range-of-motion play and for partner games.

  • Pass-throughs: wide grip, take the stick overhead and behind, slowly.
  • Hold it overhead during slow squats.
  • Partner game: one person swings the stick low and slow, the other steps and ducks around it.

Budget / DIY: A broom handle, or a hardwood dowel about 4 ft long from a hardware store. No need to buy a branded stick.

Practice ball (DIY) Researched

Why it earns a spot: A ball on a cord, swung by a partner, turns reaction and coordination into a game. The idea comes from Fighting Monkey's practice ball.

  • A partner swings the ball in slow arcs; the other moves out of its path.
  • Trade turns swinging and dodging, speed up only when it feels easy.
  • Catch the cord or ball on the swing, gently.

Budget / DIY: Thread a length of cord through a tennis ball and knot it. It needs a partner and a little instruction, so treat it as a later add.

Portable speaker Researched

Why it earns a spot: For group sessions, music sets the mood and still lets people talk, which earbuds don't. Keep the volume considerate in shared spaces.

  • A warm-up playlist for a group in the park.
  • Rounds timed to songs: work for one track, rest for the next.
  • Low background music for a garage session.

Budget / DIY: A phone speaker is fine for a solo session.
